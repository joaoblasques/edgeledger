Two tracks, run in parallel across the twelve months.

Overview Methodology Results Learning

Building the system and learning the theory happen together, roughly six hours a week combined. Track A asks whether an edge is real and how much to bet. Track B asks where a probability actually comes from. The rungs are sequenced so the theory arrives just before the code needs it.

Concept notes are written as each idea is actually learned, rather than pre-created — so the gaps in these tables are an honest picture of where the work has and hasn't reached.

Notes written so far

  • A1 — Probability Foundations

    What is a fair price? Conditional probability, Bayes, expectation and variance, odds ↔ probability ↔ log-odds, and removing the vig to recover what the market actually believes. Worked examples throughout, with answers.

  • B1 — What a Model Is

    Why does a model beat a guess? Features to probability, generative vs discriminative, signal vs noise, the bias-variance trade-off, and why log-odds is the natural working space for binary event pricing.

Track A — Probability & Inference

"Is my edge real, and how much do I bet?"

MonthsRungCore questionKey concepts
1–2A1. Probability foundationsWhat is a fair price? Conditional probability, Bayes, expectation, variance, odds↔probability↔log-odds, vig/overround removal
3–4A2. Distributions & calibrationAre my probabilities honest? Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, normal; Brier score, log loss, reliability diagrams, calibration vs discrimination
5–6A3. Statistical inferenceIs my edge luck? MLE, sampling distributions, confidence intervals, bootstrap, hypothesis testing, multiple-comparison correction, effective sample size

Track B — Modelling Foundations

"Where does a probability actually come from?"

MonthsRungCore questionKey concepts
1–2B1. What a model isWhy does a model beat a guess? Features → probability mapping, generative vs discriminative, signal vs noise, bias-variance decomposition, why log-odds is the natural space for binary event pricing
3–4B2. Baselines that are hard to beatWhat am I competing against? Market price as baseline, historical base rates, Elo, Bradley-Terry paired-comparison models. Rule: never build a model without beating a baseline first.
5–6B3. RegressionHow do I map features to probability? Linear → logistic regression, link functions, coefficient interpretation in log-odds, regularisation (L1/L2), feature scaling and encoding
6–7B4. Process modelsHow do I model the mechanism, not the outcome? Poisson and negative binomial scoring processes, Dixon-Coles adjustment, simulation from a fitted process to derive any derived market
7–8B5. Bayesian & hierarchicalHow do I handle small samples? Priors and posteriors, conjugate updating, partial pooling and shrinkage for team and player effects, uncertainty quantification, credible intervals
9–10B6. Validation & overfittingIs this backtest a lie? Time-series cross-validation, walk-forward, look-ahead leakage, survivorship bias, deflated Sharpe, backtest overfitting, out-of-sample discipline
11–12B7. Ensembling & recalibrationHow do I combine models and the market? Model blending, logarithmic opinion pooling, shrinking toward market price, Platt scaling, isotonic regression, stacking

B6 is the highest-value rung for this project's specific risk. An AI coding agent will cheerfully hand you a beautiful backtest built on leakage; B6 is what stops that, and it is why the point-in-time contract is enforced in code and tests rather than left to discipline.
